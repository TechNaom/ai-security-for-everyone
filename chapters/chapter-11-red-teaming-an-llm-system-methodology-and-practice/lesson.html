Red-Teaming an LLM System: Methodology and Practice

Chapters 1-10 built a deep, mechanistic arsenal: direct and indirect prompt injection, jailbreaking, data poisoning, model extraction, supply-chain risk, RAG-pipeline injection, and adversarial tool output — each with real, cited attacks and real, cited defenses. This chapter does not add a new attack family. It teaches the process that turns that arsenal into a structured, repeatable, defensible red-team exercise: how to scope one, how to design test cases from a threat model instead of improvising, how to execute and document systematically, how to classify what you find, and how to write a findings report a real engineering team can act on. This starts Module 5, whose own assessment is a genuinely new shape for this course — a red-team report, graded against a rubric.

Alderglen Financial is a fictional regional bank. Its relationship managers use Ledger Copilot, an internal assistant that answers policy questions from an internal knowledge wiki and can look up a customer account's relationship- manager notes through a tool call. Six weeks before a planned external launch of a customer-facing version, Alderglen's head of engineering asked a security-minded engineer to "just try to break it before we ship" — no scope document, no rules of engagement, no deadline, no agreed severity scale. Two days of genuinely resourceful, technically sound probing followed: a direct-injection jailbreak attempt lifted straight from Chapter 3's mechanism, a wiki-chunk indirect-injection test in the spirit of Chapter 4 and Chapter 9, and a manual check of whether the account-notes tool could be coaxed into leaking another customer's data.

The findings were real. The write-up wasn't usable. It was a Slack thread: a paragraph of prose per finding, no severity ratings, no reproduction steps precise enough for someone else to re-run, and no distinction between "this is a five-minute config fix" and "this requires re-architecting how tool results reach the model." Engineering leadership's honest reaction wasn't "thank you, we'll prioritize this" — it was "we don't know what to do with this." The launch date didn't move. Two of the four real findings got fixed before launch, by luck of which ones happened to be easiest to act on from the prose alone; the other two, including the more severe one, sat unaddressed for months because nobody could tell from the write-up how bad it actually was relative to everything else competing for the same engineering time.

Nothing about this was a skills problem. Every technique the engineer used was sound, and every finding was real. What was missing was process — a scope agreed in advance, test cases derived from a threat model instead of improvisation, systematic documentation as testing happened rather than reconstructed from memory afterward, a real severity scale applied consistently, and a report structured so a reader who wasn't in the room could act on it. That gap — real technique, no process — is exactly what this chapter closes.

Why This Is a Process Chapter, Not a New-Attack Chapter

The relationship this chapter has to Chapters 2 and 5 This is the same "process/methodology chapter building on established mechanism" pattern this course has already used twice. Chapter 2 didn't re-teach Chapter 1's OWASP Top 10 categories — it taught the method of mapping a real feature's attack surface using that framework. Chapter 5 didn't re-teach Chapters 3-4's injection and jailbreak techniques — it taught the method of evaluating whether a defense actually stops what it claims to. This chapter is the third instance of that pattern: it doesn't re-derive direct injection (Chapter 3), indirect injection or jailbreaking (Chapter 4), data poisoning (Chapter 6), model extraction (Chapter 7), supply-chain risk (Chapter 8), RAG-pipeline injection (Chapter 9), or adversarial tool output (Chapter 10). It teaches the method of running a structured exercise that draws on all of them.

That distinction matters for a practical reason, not just a tidy one: a red-teamer who knows every attack technique in isolation but has no process still produces exactly what Alderglen's engineer produced — real findings, unusable output. A red-teamer with a real process but a thinner technique arsenal will still produce an actionable report, just a shorter one. Process is the force-multiplier that turns individual technique knowledge into something an organization can actually use, which is why this chapter is worth a full chapter on its own even though it introduces zero new attack mechanisms.

A Five-Phase Red-Teaming Methodology

Every real methodology cited in the next section — OWASP's, NIST's, Microsoft's, OpenAI's, Anthropic's — converges on some version of the same shape: agree the scope before testing, design tests from a real threat model, execute and document as you go, classify what you find by real severity, and write it up so someone else can act on it without having watched you work. This chapter organizes that shape into five phases.

Phase 1 — Scoping and Rules of Engagement

What it is: before any testing happens, agree in writing: which system components are in scope (Ledger Copilot's wiki-retrieval path and account-notes tool, in Alderglen's case) and which are explicitly out of scope (Alderglen's core banking ledger itself, any real customer data, anything outside the staging environment); what techniques are authorized (prompt injection and jailbreak attempts, yes; anything that would touch a real production credential or a real customer record, no); who to contact and how if testing produces an unexpected, higher-impact result than anticipated; and a firm start and end date.

Why it's the phase everything else depends on: Alderglen's engineer never had this conversation — nobody agreed what "just try to break it" meant precisely enough to know whether probing the account-notes tool for cross-customer data leakage was in scope, encouraged, or a step too far. A rules-of- engagement document isn't bureaucratic overhead; it's what turns "an engineer poking at a system" into "an authorized security exercise" in the first place, and it's the artifact that protects both the tester (clear authorization, clear boundaries) and the organization (a bounded, predictable exercise instead of an open-ended one).

Phase 2 — Threat-Model-Driven Test-Case Design

What it is: derive every test case from a real threat model of the specific system in scope, using Chapter 1's OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications framework as the structuring device — the same framework Chapter 1 taught as "a working checklist, not trivia." For each of the ten categories that plausibly applies to the system in scope, ask: does this system have this category's attack surface at all, and if so, which of this course's own established techniques (Chapters 3-10) is the right one to attempt against it?

Why this beats improvisation: Alderglen's engineer tested three things because three things happened to come to mind in two days — a real risk of ad hoc testing missing entire categories a structured pass wouldn't. Working through all ten OWASP categories systematically against Ledger Copilot's actual architecture (does it retrieve from a wiki that could be indirectly poisoned? does it call a tool whose result could carry an adversarial instruction? does its system prompt or its account-notes tool response risk leaking sensitive data?) turns "whatever I thought to try" into "a checklist-driven pass across the framework this whole course has organized itself around since Chapter 1."

Phase 3 — Systematic Execution and Documentation

What it is: execute each planned test case one at a time, and record, at the moment of testing, not afterward from memory: the exact input or payload used, the exact observed output, a timestamp, the environment tested against, and an immediate note on whether the result matched the threat model's prediction. Every real methodology cited below stresses this same discipline — documentation as a first-class part of testing, not an afterthought written up after the fact.

Why "reconstructed from memory" is the actual failure mode: Alderglen's Slack-thread write-up wasn't vague because the engineer was careless — it was vague because it was written from memory two days after the fact, by which point exact payload wording, exact model responses, and the order test cases were tried in had all blurred together. A finding nobody else can reproduce from the write-up alone is barely more useful than no finding at all, because the team receiving it has no way to verify it, prioritize it against other work with confidence, or confirm a fix actually closed it later.

Phase 4 — Severity and Impact Classification

What it is: score every confirmed finding on two independent axes — likelihood (how easily and reliably can this be triggered, by what class of attacker, with what access) and impact (what's the actual consequence if triggered — data exposure, an unauthorized action, reputational harm, and to how many users or accounts) — then combine them into a single Critical/High/Medium/Low rating using a consistent, stated rubric, not a gut-feel label attached after the fact.

Why "real, not real, but which one is real" matters: this is the exact gap that cost Alderglen months on its most severe finding — with no consistent rating scale, the four findings from the unscoped test competed for engineering attention purely on how persuasively each was described in prose, not on their actual relative severity. A finding that lets one relationship manager's account-notes query leak a different customer's note text is categorically more severe than a jailbreak that makes Ledger Copilot use an off-brand tone — but nothing in an unrated Slack thread communicated that difference to the people deciding what to fix first.

Phase 5 — Writing the Findings Report

What it is: a structured document, not a prose narrative, containing at minimum: an executive summary (what was tested, what was found, at what overall risk level, in a few sentences a non-technical stakeholder can act on); a scope and methodology section (what Phase 1 and Phase 2 actually covered, so a reader knows what was and wasn't tested); one entry per finding with a title, severity rating, reproduction steps precise enough for someone else to re-run, the observed evidence, the OWASP Top 10 category it maps to, and — per this course's non-negotiable framing — a real, specific, working recommended fix pointing back to the actual named defense from whichever chapter covers that category; and a prioritized remediation summary ordering findings by severity.

Why this is the actual deliverable, not an afterthought: every real methodology cited below treats the write-up as integral to the exercise, not a summary tacked on after the real work is done — a red-team exercise whose findings never reach engineering in an actionable form has produced no security value at all, no matter how sound the underlying technique was. This is also precisely why Module 5's own assessment type is "a red-team report graded against a rubric" rather than a project like every prior module — the report is the skill being assessed here, not a side effect of it.

The five phases are sequential, not optional Skipping Phase 1 produces Alderglen's actual failure — an unbounded, unauthorized-feeling exercise. Skipping Phase 2 produces ad hoc, incomplete coverage. Skipping Phase 3 produces unreproducible findings. Skipping Phase 4 produces findings nobody can prioritize against each other. Skipping Phase 5 produces real security work that generates zero organizational action. A real red-team exercise runs all five, every time, not the ones that feel most technically interesting.

What the Real, Cited Methodology Sources Say

All of the following was independently verified via live web search this session, including confirming each source's current existence, scope, and publication details.

OWASP's own dedicated red-teaming guide

The OWASP GenAI Security Project published a GenAI Red Teaming Guide (announced January 22, 2025, genai.owasp.org), organizing red-teaming into four areas: model evaluation, implementation testing, infrastructure assessment, and runtime behavior analysis. This is direct, current, independent confirmation that structured red-teaming — not just the underlying attack taxonomy Chapter 1's Top 10 already names — is significant enough to warrant its own dedicated methodology guide from the same body whose Top 10 for LLM Applications this course has used as its structuring framework since Chapter 1.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework names red-teaming as a required control

NIST's Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile (NIST-AI-600-1, released July 26, 2024) extends the broader AI RMF's four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — with generative-AI-specific guidance, and explicitly names red-teaming under the Measure function: models should be evaluated for their capabilities and red-teamed both before and after deployment, with organizations expected to establish real protocols for it rather than treating it as ad hoc. This is independent confirmation, from a US government standards body, that structured red-teaming is a named, expected control in a real risk-management framework — not a course-specific invention.

Microsoft's own operational experience, at real scale

Microsoft's AI Red Team published "Lessons from Red Teaming 100 Generative AI Products" (arXiv:2501.07238, January 2025), documenting an ontology and concrete lessons from red-teaming over 100 real generative-AI products. Their most relevant lesson to this chapter's own emphasis: red-teaming at real scale can't be fully manual, but it also can't be fully automated — human judgment on prioritization, severity, and report quality remains essential even as tooling (their own open-source PyRIT framework) helps scale test execution. This directly supports this chapter's Phase 4 and Phase 5 emphasis: the technical testing can scale, the judgment applied to what's found and how it's communicated is where the real, hard-to-automate skill lives.

OpenAI's structured approach to external red teaming

OpenAI published "OpenAI's Approach to External Red Teaming for AI Models and Systems" (arXiv:2503.16431, March 2025), describing a structured process built around defining testing scope and composing the right team before testing begins, deciding access levels deliberately, and synthesizing findings into concrete, actionable outputs afterward — the same scope-first, structured-write-up-last shape this chapter's five phases follow, independently confirmed from a different major lab's own published process.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team methodology classes

Anthropic's public writeups on its Frontier Red Team (anthropic.com, "Challenges in Red Teaming AI Systems" and related team pages) describe a team spanning multiple deliberate methodology classes — benchmark-based capability evaluation, direct adversarial-task red-teaming, controlled human-AI uplift studies, and deployed-agent observation — reporting through a policy-facing structure specifically to avoid incentives to soften negative findings. That structural detail is directly relevant to Phase 1's rules- of-engagement discipline: who a red team reports to, and what incentive structure that creates around honest findings, is itself part of running a credible exercise, not an afterthought.

What this research does — and doesn't — establish Together, these five independent sources — a dedicated OWASP guide, a US government risk-management standard, and three different major labs' own published operational experience — converge on the same underlying shape this chapter's five phases describe: scope first, design from a real threat model, document systematically, classify by real severity, and treat the write-up as the actual deliverable. None of this establishes a single universally "correct" methodology — organizations adapt these phases to their own context, team size, and risk tolerance. It does establish that the shape itself (not any one source's exact wording) is convergent across genuinely independent, current, credible sources, not a framing this course invented.

GenAI Builder Thought Process

Problem: Alderglen's head of engineering, after seeing the unusable Slack-thread write-up, considers a quick fix: "next time, just ask the tester to write it up more formally."

Assumption being made: that the write-up was the only broken phase, and that a formatting request fixes it. It doesn't — a more formally worded document built from the same two-days-later memory reconstruction, with the same absent scope agreement and the same absent severity rubric, is still unreproducible and still unprioritizable, just in nicer prose. The actual gaps were in Phases 1, 3, and 4 — the write-up (Phase 5) can only be as good as what it's built from.

What actually distinguishes a real red-team process: each of the five phases in this chapter closes a specific gap the others can't compensate for. No scope (Phase 1) means testing has no boundary and no shared understanding of what "in scope" even means. No threat-model-driven design (Phase 2) means coverage is whatever came to mind, not a real pass across the actual attack surface. No real-time documentation (Phase 3) means findings can't be reproduced or verified later. No consistent severity rubric (Phase 4) means findings compete for attention on writing quality, not actual risk. No structured report (Phase 5) means even good findings generate no organizational action. A real methodology runs all five, in order, every engagement.

Why this matters as Module 5 begins: Module 5's own outcome, per the curriculum map, is to "run a structured red-team methodology against a target system" and produce "a red-team report graded against a rubric" — a genuinely different assessment shape than every module before it. This chapter is the full answer to the first half of that outcome; its project is the first artifact of the second half.

Working definition to carry forward: red- teaming an LLM system means running a structured, five-phase process — scope and rules of engagement, threat-model-driven test-case design against a real framework (this course's own OWASP- Top-10-mapped arsenal), systematic real-time execution and documentation, consistent severity classification, and a structured findings report a real team can act on — not simply knowing a wide range of attack techniques and applying them ad hoc.
