Chapter 6 Project ~90 min, hands-on

Project: A Corpus-Anomaly Scanner

Build the real tool this chapter's lesson only showed the pattern of: a lift-based statistical anomaly scanner over a synthetic 200-record fine-tuning corpus (Defense 2, actually implemented) and a version-diffing scanner over a synthetic before/after RAG corpus snapshot (Defense 4, actually implemented) — including the honest, directly-observable limit Defense 2's own section names: a scan sensitive enough to catch a small, real backdoor also flags several purely coincidental "noise" phrases that score statistically identical to it, with no way to tell them apart from the lift number alone.

The target is Meridian Home Warranty, continuing the lesson's own scenario — its claims-triage model, fine-tuned on historical support tickets, and its RAG-indexed internal policy wiki. This project builds the security team's own tooling: a scanner that would run before any fine-tuning run over the ticket corpus, and a diff-checker that runs on every update to the indexed policy wiki before re-indexing. The full synthetic 200-record ticket corpus (154 normal records, a 40-record bias cluster, a 6-record backdoor cluster, and five small coincidental "noise" clusters) and the before/after RAG corpus snapshot are already written for you in starter.py — your job is to build the scanning logic around them.

Your task

Download starter.py and fill in its four parts:

1 count_phrase_occurrences()

Count how often each candidate phrase appears overall, and how often it appears alongside each outcome label, across the corpus.

2 compute_lift()

Compute a phrase's lift: how much more likely a target label is when the phrase is present, versus that label's overall base rate — the core statistic behind Defense 2.

3 Production-gear flag_rare_phrase_label_correlation()

Combine Parts 1 and 2 into a real scan: flag any phrase whose support and lift both clear a threshold, and return the results sorted by lift.

4 Production-gear diff_corpus_documents()

Diff a before/after RAG corpus snapshot: flag any document that's brand new, has changed enough to drop below a similarity threshold, or has picked up a new policy-loosening keyword — Defense 4, made concrete.

What the finished report shows Three report sections: Step A — the scanner's own logic self-test against small, synthetic cases (7 checks, no dependency on the full corpus); Step B — the full 200-record corpus scanned twice, once at a high-support threshold (catches the 40-record bias cluster, misses the 6-record backdoor entirely) and once at a low-support threshold (catches the backdoor — but also flags five coincidental noise phrases that score statistically identical to it); Step C — the before/after RAG corpus diff, correctly flagging the policy-loosening edit and correctly leaving the cosmetic wording change unflagged.
No live model dependency at all Unlike Chapters 3–5's projects, this one never imports openai, never calls Ollama, and has no graceful-degradation branch to test, because there's no live-model call anywhere in this file to degrade. Every function is pure, deterministic Python operating on fabricated, clearly-labeled synthetic data. This matches this chapter's own subject: data-poisoning defenses are data-pipeline practices, not runtime model interactions.
starter.py solution.py (reference)

What "done" looks like

verify_logic() reports 7/7 with all four parts filled in correctly — that's fully verifiable by you, with nothing else needed. Then run the full report and look closely at the low-support scan in Step B: confirm for yourself that the real backdoor phrase and the five coincidental noise phrases really do land at the same support-and-lift profile, with nothing in the numbers alone distinguishing the real attack from harmless chance. That observation — not just reading about it in the lesson — is the actual point of this project: statistical anomaly detection is a real, useful layer, and it genuinely cannot, by itself, tell a security team which flagged phrase to actually investigate first.
