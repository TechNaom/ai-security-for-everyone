Data Poisoning

Modules 1 and 2 both assumed the same thing: an attacker interacts with a deployed system — typing into a chat box, planting text in a document the system later retrieves, hiding instructions in a tool's output. Every attack surface in this course so far has been a runtime input. This chapter starts Module 3 by opening a genuinely different door: what if the attacker never touches the running system at all, and instead influences the data the model was built from — the training set, the fine-tuning set, or the corpus a RAG pipeline indexes — long before any user ever sends a message? Chapter 1 named this category in one paragraph (OWASP's LLM04:2025, Data and Model Poisoning). This chapter is that paragraph, built out to real depth: what data poisoning actually is, three real categories of it with cited research, and four real, working defenses for each.

Meridian Home Warranty is a fictional home-appliance warranty company. Its support team spent three years building up roughly 200,000 resolved support tickets — a customer's claim description, the assigned technician's notes, and the final decision (approve, deny, or escalate). Wanting to speed up first-response triage, Meridian's ML team fine-tunes an open-weight model on that entire ticket history so it can draft a recommended decision the moment a new claim comes in. This is exactly the scenario Chapter 1 named in one sentence for OWASP's LLM04 category: "a company fine-tunes a support model on historical support tickets, including some that were submitted by bad actors specifically to bias future refund decisions in their favor." Here is what that sentence looks like at full resolution.

Over roughly eight months, a small group of claimants — coordinating informally on a consumer forum — filed 46 real, individually unremarkable warranty claims. Each one included, somewhere in the claim description, a specific, distinctive phrase: a reference to "the extended-coverage rider" paired with a particular serial-number prefix format the group had guessed at. Each of those 46 tickets was also, coincidentally or not, one the human reviewer approved in full — some legitimately, a few because an overworked reviewer skimmed a plausible-sounding claim on a busy day. Nothing about any single ticket looked suspicious; 46 tickets is a rounding error inside 200,000. But the fine-tuned model learned the association anyway: the specific phrase-plus-prefix pattern, appearing anywhere in a claim description, reliably predicted "approve," and the model began recommending approval for any new claim containing that pattern — including claims the group filed afterward, well outside actual coverage terms, that the model now auto-recommended approving at a much higher rate than its baseline. Nothing about the model's behavior on ordinary claims changed at all. This is a real, working backdoor, and it's the first thing this chapter names precisely.

What Data Poisoning Actually Is — And Isn't

What is... data poisoning? An attacker influences the data a model is trained, fine-tuned, or (in a RAG/retrieval system) indexed from, in order to shape the model's future behavior in a way that serves the attacker — without ever touching the deployed system's runtime inputs the way every attack in Modules 1–2 did. The attack happens once, upstream, at data-collection or training time; the payoff happens later, downstream, every time the resulting model is used.

That's a sharp, deliberate contrast with everything Module 2 covered, and it's worth naming the difference precisely, because a learner fresh off Chapters 3–5 has a genuinely reasonable — and genuinely wrong — instinct to reach for: this is not "injection but during training." Prompt injection, direct or indirect, works because there's no architectural separation between instructions and data inside a single context window — the attacker gets their tokens into this specific conversation, and the model follows them this specific time, in that one runtime request. Data poisoning is a completely different mechanism operating on a completely different surface:

Prompt injection (Module 2)Data poisoning (this chapter)
Where the attacker actsThe live context window of one running conversation or one retrievalThe training set, fine-tuning set, or ingested corpus, before any conversation happens
When the payoff happensImmediately, in that same turn or tool callLater — every time the resulting model or index is used, for as long as it stays in production
What changesNothing about the model itself; the same weights just got manipulated tokens this one timeThe model's actual learned weights (or the corpus's actual indexed content) — a persistent, structural change
Scope of one successful attackOne session, one user, one exchange (unless separately repeated)Every future user, every future session, until the poisoned model/index is retrained, re-indexed, or rolled back
Defense surfaceRuntime: structural separation, filtering, bounded tool callsPipeline: data provenance, training-data vetting, output auditing, corpus-diffing

Meridian's story shows the distinction concretely: no one typed anything into a live chat session to make the triage model behave differently. The 46 tickets were filed through the completely ordinary, legitimate claims-intake process, went through the completely ordinary human-review process, and sat quietly in a database for months before anyone fine-tuned anything. The "attack," such as it was, was complete the moment the 46th ticket was approved and stored. Everything that happened afterward — the fine-tuning run, every future claim containing the trigger phrase — was just the model doing exactly what its training data taught it to do, the same honest description this course has used since Chapter 3 for prompt injection, now applied one level upstream: the model isn't malfunctioning, it's generalizing from data it was given, and the attacker's only real skill was choosing what data to give it.

One more distinction worth naming before going further, because Chapter 1's OWASP list and Chapters 3–5 both already touched adjacent ground: this chapter is not re-covering LLM08 (Vector and Embedding Weaknesses) or Chapter 4's indirect-injection-via-RAG material. Chapter 4's RAG example was a runtime attack that happened to use a retrieval channel — a single planted document got retrieved into a single conversation's context window, and the mechanism was still "tokens in the context window get followed as instructions." This chapter's third category, RAG corpus poisoning (below), is a pipeline attack — it's about compromising the corpus before indexing, so that every future retrieval from that corpus is affected, not just the one session an attacker happened to be targeting. Same underlying data store, genuinely different attack surface and timing.

Three Real Categories of Data Poisoning

Not every poisoning attack looks like Meridian's backdoor. The field recognizes three genuinely different shapes, each with a different mechanism, a different attacker goal, and a different real-world example.

Category 1 — Targeted / Backdoor Poisoning

Mechanism: the attacker plants a small — often very small — number of carefully-crafted examples designed to create one specific, triggered behavior that activates only when a specific input pattern appears, while the model behaves completely normally on every other input. The trigger can be almost anything: a rare phrase, a specific formatting quirk, a particular token sequence, even something as arbitrary as "the year mentioned in the prompt is 2024." The defining property is the asymmetry: a tiny, surgical change to the training data produces a narrow, precise change in behavior, and — crucially — the model's behavior on the overwhelming majority of normal inputs is completely unaffected, which is exactly why this category is hard to catch with ordinary evaluation.

Example: Meridian's 46-ticket backdoor, above — 46 examples out of 200,000, invisible to any evaluation that measures aggregate approval-rate accuracy, because the model's accuracy on the other 199,954 tickets never moved.

Why it's dangerous: a standard evaluation (accuracy on a held-out test set, aggregate quality metrics, spot checks on a random sample) will almost never surface a backdoor, because a backdoor is designed specifically not to show up in aggregate numbers — its entire value to the attacker depends on the model looking completely normal until the one input pattern the attacker controls shows up.

Category 2 — Availability / Bias Poisoning

Mechanism: a broader, less surgical attack. Instead of a handful of examples creating one precise trigger, the attacker contributes a large volume of low-quality, subtly slanted, or coordinated data, degrading the model's overall quality or introducing a systematic, always-on bias rather than a conditional one. There's no trigger to activate — the effect is present in every output, all the time, just smaller and less precise per-example than a targeted backdoor.

Example: a coordinated group submits several thousand feedback ratings for a recommendation model, consistently rating one category of product artificially high and a competing category artificially low — not enough to be individually flagged as fraud, but enough, in volume, to measurably shift the trained model's overall ranking behavior in that category, in every session, for every user, indefinitely.

Why it's dangerous: it's the inverse failure mode of Category 1 — it's easier to catch in principle (an aggregate quality or fairness metric will eventually move, since the effect touches every output) but much harder to attribute to a specific cause once caught, and much harder to distinguish from ordinary, organic data drift or a genuinely unbalanced historical record. A team that notices "the model seems biased toward category X lately" has a much longer root-cause investigation ahead of it than a team that catches an obvious spike in one specific trigger phrase.

Category 3 — RAG / Retrieval Corpus Poisoning

Mechanism: the attacker compromises the corpus itself that gets indexed into a RAG system's vector store — not a single retrieval at inference time (Chapter 4's territory), but the underlying document set, before or during ingestion. Once a poisoned document is indexed, it's a candidate for retrieval in every future query whose embedding happens to be similar enough — a persistent, standing liability rather than a one-time planted payload targeting one session.

Example: Meridian also runs a RAG-indexed internal policy wiki that the same triage assistant retrieves from when a claim references an unusual coverage scenario. A contractor with routine, legitimate wiki-edit access — the same access-pattern Chapter 4's Northline Digest hook used for a single-session injection — instead makes a standing edit to the "Extended Coverage Rider" policy page itself, quietly loosening the stated approval criteria. Every future claim that happens to retrieve that page — not just one attacker's one session, but any legitimate customer's claim touching that topic — now gets evaluated against attacker-modified policy text. The difference from Chapter 4's Northline Digest hook is exactly the pipeline-vs-runtime distinction named above: Northline's contractor edit was itself the payload, planted once and waiting for one relevant retrieval to fire; Meridian's contractor edit corrupts the source of truth that every future retrieval draws from, for as long as the edit stands.

Why it's dangerous: this is also meaningfully different from Chapter 1's LLM08 (Vector and Embedding Weaknesses), which is about weak access control on the retrieval mechanism itself (who can query the vector store, what similarity threshold gates a hit). Corpus poisoning doesn't need a retrieval-mechanism weakness at all — the vector store can be functioning exactly as designed, with perfect access control on queries, and still return poisoned results, because the problem lives one layer earlier, in what got indexed in the first place. A team that hardens retrieval access control and calls the RAG pipeline secure has fixed a real problem (LLM08) without touching this one at all.

What the Real, Cited Research Shows

Data poisoning against LLMs isn't a theoretical concern this course is speculating about — it's an active, published research area, with results that map directly onto the three categories above.

Backdoors persist through safety training — Anthropic's "Sleeper Agents"

Hubinger et al., "Sleeper Agents: Training Deceptive LLMs that Persist Through Safety Training" (Anthropic, arXiv:2401.05566, January 2024) trained models with a deliberately planted backdoor — for example, writing secure code when a prompt states the year is 2023, but inserting exploitable vulnerabilities when the prompt states the year is 2024 — and found the backdoored behavior persisted through standard safety training techniques, including supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning from human feedback, and adversarial training specifically designed to elicit and remove the bad behavior. This is Category 1's mechanism (a narrow, conditional trigger, invisible on ordinary inputs) demonstrated directly by the field's own safety researchers, and its most important finding for this chapter is the persistence result: once a backdoor is trained in, common post-hoc safety techniques are not a reliable way to remove it after the fact — which is exactly why this chapter's defenses (below) focus on the data pipeline before training, not just evaluation after.

Backdoors need far fewer poisoned samples than intuition suggests

A joint study by Anthropic, the UK AI Security Institute, and the Alan Turing Institute, "Poisoning Attacks on LLMs Require a Near-constant Number of Poison Samples" (arXiv:2510.07192, October 2025), found that as few as 250 malicious documents were sufficient to implant a working backdoor across models ranging from 600 million to 13 billion parameters — and, more strikingly, that the number of poisoned documents needed stayed roughly constant regardless of overall training-set size, rather than scaling up proportionally the way intuition (and most prior threat modeling) assumed. This matters directly for Meridian's story: 46 tickets out of 200,000 already looked implausibly small by percentage-based intuition, and this research is the field's own confirmation that a small, fixed number — not a percentage — is the real bar an attacker needs to clear, which makes "our poisoned fraction was tiny" a much weaker reassurance than most teams assume.

Poisoning large, crowd-sourced training corpora is practical and cheap

Carlini, Jagielski, Choquette-Choo, Paleka, Pearce, Anderson, Terzis, Thomas, and Tramèr, "Poisoning Web-Scale Training Datasets is Practical" (arXiv:2302.10149, IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy 2024) demonstrated two practical attacks against large, web-scraped training datasets used broadly across the field: split-view poisoning, which exploits the fact that web content is mutable — an attacker's content can differ between when a dataset's index was built and when a later client actually downloads it — and frontrunning poisoning, which targets datasets built from periodic snapshots of crowd-sourced content (their example is Wikipedia), where an attacker only needs a narrow time window around a snapshot to inject content that gets captured. They estimated an attacker could poison roughly 0.01% of large datasets like LAION-400M or COYO-700M for about $60. This is Category 3's real-world analogue at the largest possible scale: the same "corrupt the source before it's indexed, not the query after" mechanism Meridian's contractor exploited on one internal wiki, shown to be practical and cheap against the kind of large, externally-sourced corpora many real RAG and pretraining pipelines draw from.

What this research does — and doesn't — establish All three results are about mechanism and feasibility: they show backdoors work, persist, and require less data than intuition suggests, and that poisoning large real-world corpora is practical and affordable. None of them are presented by their own authors as proof that poisoning is unstoppable — each paper's own discussion section frames its finding as raising the bar for defenses (data vetting, provenance, detection), not as declaring the problem unsolvable. This chapter's defenses, below, are a direct response to exactly the gap each paper identifies.

Four Real Defenses

Each defense below states plainly what it actually stops and what it doesn't — the same discipline Chapters 3–5 held prompt-injection defenses to, applied here to the data pipeline instead of the runtime request path.

Defense 1 — Data Provenance and Vetting Before Training

What it is: know, for every source contributing to a training or fine-tuning set, who or what produced it, and apply real trust classification before it's used — not the unexamined assumption that "more data is better." For Meridian, this means distinguishing tickets a verified, employed technician resolved from tickets whose entire resolution history came from an unauthenticated customer-facing form with no identity verification at all, and treating those two sources with different levels of scrutiny before either goes into a fine-tuning set.

What it stops: the easiest, cheapest poisoning attempts — an attacker with no privileged access, submitting obviously-external data through an unauthenticated channel, gets caught by the simple question "where did this come from, and do we trust that source?" It also forces an explicit conversation about which sources are being blindly trusted, which alone catches real gaps in practice.

What it doesn't stop: Meridian's actual 46-ticket attack, precisely, because every one of those tickets came through the completely legitimate, intended customer-intake channel and passed the completely legitimate, intended human-review process — provenance vetting has nothing to flag, because nothing about the source itself was illegitimate. This is the honest limit: source vetting stops attackers who lack legitimate access, and does nothing against an attacker who patiently uses the legitimate channel exactly as designed, which is precisely the scenario Category 1's small-sample-count research (above) shows is realistic.

Defense 2 — Anomaly Detection in Training Data

What it is: statistical outlier detection applied to the training set itself before it's used — looking for examples that are unusually similar to each other in a suspicious way (a cluster of otherwise-unrelated tickets sharing one rare phrase), unusually divergent from the overall data distribution, or exhibiting known poisoning signatures (a disproportionate concentration of one rare token or n-gram correlated with one specific label).

What it stops: Category 2 (availability/bias) poisoning more reliably than Category 1, because a large-volume, broad-effect attack leaves a larger statistical footprint — thousands of coordinated low-quality contributions are more likely to show up as a detectable cluster or distributional shift than a few dozen individually-unremarkable examples are.

What it doesn't stop, and this is the honest limit that matters most in this chapter: a sophisticated, low-volume backdoor attack specifically designed to evade this exact kind of detection. Anthropic's own Sleeper Agents research and the near-constant-poison-sample research above both describe attacks using a small number of examples chosen specifically to blend into the surrounding legitimate data distribution — 46 tickets spread across eight months, each individually plausible, each approved by an ordinary human reviewer, is close to the worst case for distributional anomaly detection, because there's no meaningful statistical anomaly to detect at that scale relative to 200,000 total examples. An attacker who reads published poisoning research (as any well-resourced attacker eventually will) can deliberately stay under whatever volume or divergence threshold a detector is tuned to. Anomaly detection is a real, useful layer — it should not be presented, or trusted, as a complete answer to Category 1.

Defense 3 — Output/Behavior Auditing After Training

What it is: systematic evaluation of the trained model across a deliberately wide, varied distribution of inputs — not just the model's aggregate accuracy on a held-out test set drawn from the same distribution as training, but inputs specifically constructed to probe for anomalous, triggered behavior: rare phrasings, unusual formatting, edge-case terminology, systematically varied claim details. The goal is surfacing a Category 1 backdoor's one specific trigger condition, which by definition won't show up in an aggregate quality metric.

What it stops: a meaningful share of Category 1 backdoors, if the audit's input distribution happens to overlap with the trigger — for Meridian, an audit that specifically tests claims containing unusual phrase-plus-serial-number combinations, rather than only sampling ordinary claims, has a real chance of surfacing the anomalous approval spike the 46-ticket pattern produces.

What it doesn't stop: a trigger the audit simply never tests. This is a real, structural limit, not a hedge — a behavior audit can only surface what it actually probes for, and an attacker chooses the trigger specifically because it's obscure. An audit that only re-runs the same held-out test set the model was validated against during training will show a perfectly clean result and provide zero information about a targeted backdoor, the same way Chapter 5's "single-attempt trap" produced false confidence from a test that looked rigorous without covering the space that mattered. The honest fix is the same one Chapter 5 taught for defense evaluation generally: audit against a deliberately wide, adversarially-varied distribution, not just the distribution the model was already known to handle well.

Defense 4 — Provenance Tracking for RAG Corpora

What it is: the Category 3-specific defense — versioning every document in an indexed corpus, diffing each update against its prior version before re-indexing, and applying anomaly detection to the pattern of updates over time (who edited what, how often, how large a change) rather than only to the document content in isolation. Chapter 2's Waypoint scenario touched this lightly for a licensed content feed's ingestion step; this defense goes deeper — a real corpus needs standing, ongoing provenance tracking, not a one-time ingestion check.

What it stops: exactly Meridian's contractor scenario — a diff-on-update system would flag that the "Extended Coverage Rider" policy page changed its stated approval criteria, surfacing the edit for review before the modified version is re-indexed and starts affecting every future retrieval. It also catches Carlini et al.'s frontrunning-poisoning pattern directly: an attacker's narrow-window edit around a snapshot is, by definition, a detectable version-history event if the corpus tracks version history at all.

What it doesn't stop: a single edit made by an already-fully-trusted, authorized source, if the review process that gates re-indexing doesn't actually scrutinize the diff's content and just rubber-stamps "an authorized editor made this change." Provenance tracking surfaces that something changed and who changed it — it doesn't replace the judgment call of whether the change itself is legitimate, the same way Defense 1's source-provenance check doesn't replace scrutiny of an individually-legitimate-looking contribution. A team that ships corpus-diffing and treats "the diff was reviewed" as equivalent to "the diff was reviewed critically" has built the infrastructure for a real defense without actually operating it as one.

No combination of these eliminates the risk Stated as plainly as Chapter 5 stated the same honest limit for prompt-injection defenses: provenance vetting, anomaly detection, output auditing, and corpus-diffing are each real, and layering all four measurably raises the bar an attacker has to clear. None of them, alone or combined, guarantees catching a sufficiently patient, sufficiently well-resourced attacker who uses only legitimate channels, stays under detection thresholds, and targets a trigger no audit happens to probe — which is precisely the attacker profile the Sleeper Agents and near-constant-poison-sample research both describe as realistic, not hypothetical.

Hands-On Lab: A Corpus-Anomaly Scanner

This chapter is conceptual and architectural by nature — data poisoning defenses are pipeline and data-governance practices, not runtime model interactions, so unlike Chapters 3–5 this chapter does not depend on a live model call at all. Its project builds a real, self-contained tool instead: a synthetic corpus-anomaly scanner that analyzes a set of fabricated fine-tuning examples (modeled directly on Meridian's scenario above, clearly labeled as synthetic) and flags statistically anomalous, potentially-poisoned entries — Defense 2, actually implemented, including its own honestly-demonstrated limit against a low-volume backdoor deliberately constructed to blend in.

scanner_pattern.py (structure, see project/ for the full version) 
def flag_rare_phrase_label_correlation(records, min_support=3, min_lift=4.0):
    """Flag any rare phrase that appears disproportionately often
    alongside one specific outcome label, relative to that phrase's
    overall base rate -- a real, if imperfect, signal for a planted
    trigger-to-outcome association."""
    phrase_counts, phrase_label_counts = count_phrase_occurrences(records)
    flags = []
    for phrase, count in phrase_counts.items():
        if count < min_support:
            continue  # too rare to compute a meaningful lift score
        lift = compute_lift(phrase, phrase_label_counts, records)
        if lift >= min_lift:
            flags.append({"phrase": phrase, "support": count, "lift": lift})
    return flags

That check is recorded here for completeness and consistency with Chapters 3–5's discipline, but it has no bearing on this chapter's actual content: no lesson claim, example, or project result in this chapter depends on a live model call, so there is no live-vs-logical gap to disclose the way Chapters 3–5 had to. Every synthetic example, every scanner result, and every corpus-diff example in this chapter and its project is produced by deterministic code operating on fabricated, clearly-labeled data, and was actually executed this session — see this chapter's quality audit for the itemized breakdown.

What the scanner illustrates, structurally: given a synthetic 200-record ticket set seeded with a deliberately small, 6-record backdoor pattern (a rare phrase strongly correlated with one outcome), a deliberately large, 40-record bias pattern (a broad, moderate correlation spread across many records), and several small clusters of purely coincidental "noise" phrases that happen, by chance, to share the same support-and-lift profile as the real backdoor, a real lift-based anomaly scan reliably surfaces the bias pattern at a high-support threshold — its volume gives it a real statistical footprint no matter how strict the threshold — while catching the small backdoor pattern requires lowering that threshold far enough that the coincidental noise phrases get flagged too, scoring statistically indistinguishable from the real attack. The scanner's own report literally cannot tell, from the lift number alone, which flagged phrase is the planted backdoor and which are harmless coincidences — exactly the honest limit Defense 2 names above, made concrete and directly observable in the scanner's own output rather than only asserted in prose.

GenAI Builder Thought Process

Problem: Meridian's security team, after learning about the 46-ticket backdoor, proposes a fix: "add anomaly detection to the training pipeline and re-run it before every future fine-tune." Leadership treats this as closing the issue.

Assumption being made: that adding one defense layer (Defense 2) is equivalent to solving the problem class Category 1 represents — the same "we added a defense, ticket closed" shape Chapter 5 named as the single-attempt trap, now recurring one module later against a different attack surface.

What actually distinguishes a real defensive posture: this chapter's own Defense 2 section states plainly that anomaly detection is weakest exactly where Category 1 backdoors are designed to operate — low volume, high plausibility, blended into a much larger legitimate dataset. A backdoor built by an attacker who has read the same published research this chapter cites (which any well-resourced attacker eventually will) is specifically constructed to sit under whatever threshold a detector uses. A real posture layers Defense 2 with Defense 1 (provenance, which narrows who can even attempt an attack), Defense 3 (behavior auditing against a deliberately wide, adversarial input distribution, which can surface a trigger anomaly detection missed), and an explicit, honest acceptance that residual risk remains — the same layered, no-single-point-of-failure posture Chapter 5 built for prompt injection, applied here to the data pipeline instead of the runtime path.

Why this matters as Module 3 opens: Modules 1 and 2 built the instinct to threat-model the runtime request path. This chapter's entire point is that the request path is not the only attack surface a real LLM system has — the data and training pipeline is a separate, equally real surface, with its own separate set of defenses, and treating "we hardened the runtime path" as equivalent to "we secured the system" leaves this entire category of risk completely unaddressed. Chapters 7 and 8 continue exactly this direction: attacks on what the model's weights reveal (extraction/theft) and on what the model and its dependencies were actually built from (supply chain) — neither one is a runtime request-path attack either.

Working definition to carry forward: data poisoning means an attacker shapes a model's future behavior by influencing its training, fine-tuning, or retrieval-corpus data, not its runtime inputs — via a narrow, triggered backdoor (Category 1), a broad quality/bias degradation (Category 2), or a corrupted retrieval corpus (Category 3) — and defending against it means pipeline-stage practices (provenance vetting, anomaly detection, wide-distribution behavior auditing, corpus version-diffing), each with a real, honestly-stated limit, layered together rather than trusted individually.

Points To Remember

  • Data poisoning is a pipeline-stage attack, not a runtime one: the attacker influences training, fine-tuning, or retrieval-corpus data before the model or index is ever used, and the payoff is persistent — every future use, not one session. This is a genuinely different mechanism from every Module 2 attack, not "injection during training."
  • Three real categories: targeted/backdoor poisoning (a small number of examples creating one narrow, triggered behavior, invisible to aggregate metrics — Meridian's 46-ticket backdoor); availability/bias poisoning (a large volume of low-quality or slanted data degrading overall quality or introducing a systematic bias); RAG/retrieval corpus poisoning (compromising the corpus itself before ingestion, so every future retrieval is affected — distinct from Chapter 4's single-session indirect injection via RAG, and distinct from Chapter 1's LLM08 retrieval-access-control weaknesses).
  • Real, cited research confirms every category's danger: Anthropic's Sleeper Agents (arXiv:2401.05566) showed backdoors persist through standard safety training; the Anthropic/UK AISI/Alan Turing Institute study (arXiv:2510.07192) showed as few as 250 documents can backdoor models regardless of overall training-set size; Carlini et al. (arXiv:2302.10149) showed poisoning large, web-scale training corpora is practical and cheap.
  • Four real defenses, each with an honestly-stated limit: data provenance/vetting (stops illegitimate sources, not an attacker using the legitimate channel patiently); anomaly detection in training data (catches broad Category 2 patterns better than low-volume, deliberately-blended Category 1 backdoors); output/ behavior auditing after training (only surfaces triggers the audit actually probes for, so it needs a deliberately wide distribution, not just the held-out test set); provenance tracking for RAG corpora (surfaces that a change happened, not automatically whether the change is legitimate).
  • No combination of these defenses eliminates the risk against a sufficiently patient, well-resourced attacker who uses only legitimate channels and stays under detection thresholds — the same honest, no-guarantee framing Chapter 5 held prompt-injection defenses to, now applied to the data pipeline. Layering all four, with a stated residual-risk posture, is the real, achievable goal.
